Dance: Rhythm, Pattern, and Movement in Action

Let us begin with dance. Just as in music, rhythm is fundamental to dance. Many of our local dances are performed with live music, often featuring drums or other percussion instruments that establish a strong, steady beat. This beat guides the dancers, helping them to move together in sync. Rhythm is evident in the precise timing of steps and how movements align with the music's pulse.

Pattern in dance is closely linked to rhythm. It is expressed through repeated steps, formations, and group arrangements. When dancers follow a rhythm consistently, they create a visible and palpable pattern.

Movement in dance encompasses all the actions the dancers perform, such as stepping, turning, swaying, or jumping. These movements are shaped by both rhythm and pattern, allowing the dance to tell a story or convey emotions effectively.

Let us examine some well-known local dances to see how rhythm, pattern, and movement combine to bring these traditions to life!

Sinulog

As we know, this dance originates from Cebu, Philippines. It is performed during the Sinulog Festival, a celebration honoring the Santo Niño, or the Child Jesus. As you learned earlier, the sinulog features a distinctive one-step forward, two-steps backward movement that mimics the flow of water currents. This pattern, called sinug (from the Cebuano word sug, meaning “the sea”), is a dance-prayer typically performed before the image of the Santo Niño. This key pattern distinguishes the sinulog dance from other traditional dances.

Watch again the video of the sinulog dance. (Refer to the QR code in I Can Construct.) Pay close attention to the rhythm of the music. Can you hear the steady beat of the drums? Try tapping your fingers or clapping softly in time with it. Now, observe the dancers’ feet. Do you see how their steps coordinate with the beat?

Next, notice how the dancers’ movements align with the music. Their steps, particularly the Sinug pattern, closely follow the beat. Even the flowing and graceful arm movements are synchronized with the rhythm.

Ati-Atihan

The ati-atihan dance comes from the Ati-Atihan Festival of Kalibo, Aklan, which also honors the Santo Niño.

Watch a clip of this dance. Again, focus on the rhythm and overall feel of the music. How does it compare to the sinulog music? What similarities do you notice?

Now, observe the dancers’ movements. How would you describe them? What shapes, lines, or patterns do they create with their bodies?

You will likely notice that the ati-atihan dance movements are “bigger” and more energetic, featuring a lot of foot stomping, marching, and expansive arm motions. Unlike the sinulog, the ati-atihan incorporates more freely movement, lacking a single unifying pattern across all performances. However, all movements remain strongly synchronized with the music’s beat.

Subli

The subli is a local dance from Batangas, Philippines, specifically from the towns of Bauan and Alitagtag. It originated as a sacred dance ritual dedicated to the Holy Cross of Alitagtag. The dance’s name comes from the Tagalog words subsob, meaning “to stoop or bend over,” and bali, meaning “broken.”

Watch a clip of this unique dance. Listen to the music, paying attention to the rhythm and beat. Does it sound different from the music of the previous two dances? What is the overall feel of the music? Can you identify any similarities to the sinulog and ati-atihan music?

Observe the dancers’ movements. Are there any particular shapes they create with their arms and bodies? What patterns do you notice? This partner dance differs from the group-oriented sinulog and ati-atihan.

Let us revisit the Tagalog origins of the dance’s name. Do you see any movements that relate to those words?

If you observed that the male dancers often maintain a stooped or bent-over posture, you have a keen eye! This posture is a defining characteristic of the subli dance.

The dance also includes bowing and stepping movements, with dancers gracefully turning and moving in circles. All movements are synchronized with the music’s beat.

Drama: Rhythm, Pattern, and Movement in Performance

Just like in music and dance, rhythm, pattern, and movement are essential elements in dramatic or theatrical performances. These elements enhance the storytelling and make the performance more engaging and easier to follow.

Rhythm in drama is evident in the way actors deliver their lines, whether spoken or sung. Sometimes, lines are delivered in a slow, chant-like manner, creating a specific cadence. Other times, they are sung with a steady beat, similar to a song. Background music also contributes to the rhythm of a scene, setting the emotional tone—slow and serious for sad moments, or faster and more intense for exciting scenes.

Patterns in drama are seen in the organization of the scenes, which usually follow a clear sequence, much like chapters in a book. Patterns also emerge in the actors’ movements on stage—they might march in formation, create lines, or repeat actions together for emphasis. Even the costumes and actors’ positioning can create recurring visual patterns.

Movement refers to how actors use their bodies to convey the story. These movements are often carefully planned and symbolic, such as kneeling, expressing respect, raising arms to show praise or sadness, or turning towards the audience to deliver a powerful line. Some dramas also incorporate choreography, including simple dance steps or staged combat, which are rehearsed to match the narrative.

Let us explore some traditional Filipino dramas, such as the senakulo and panunuluyan, to see how rhythm, pattern, and movement combine to bring these cultural stories to life!

Senakulo

The senakulo is a theatrical drama that originated in the mid-18th century in the Philippines. It dramatizes the suffering, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ and is typically performed during Holy Week or Lent. It can be staged in a theater or presented on the streets of a town or city. In some regions of the Philippines, the full staged version of the senakulo can extend over seven to eight days! However, shortened versions are also performed, often on Good Friday, focusing on scenes from the agony of Jesus Christ.

Watch this excerpt from a staged senakulo performance. What stands out in this senakulo excerpt in terms of rhythm, pattern, and movements?

You may have noticed the rhythmic delivery of the lines. They are often poetic and chant-like, spoken with clarity and strong emotion. This rhythm helps the actors connect with the audience on an emotional level. The accompanying music also reflects the mood of each scene—slower for solemn or sorrowful moments, and more intense for dramatic scenes.

The senakulo’s pattern follows a fixed sequence. It consistently narrates the story of Jesus—His life, death, and resurrection—and this order remains unchanged. Each year, the same key scenes are performed in the same sequence, creating a sense of tradition and familiarity.

The movements in the senakulo are highly symbolic. Each action is deliberate, designed to convey the emotions of the story. For example, an actor might fall to the ground to depict pain, raise their hands toward the heavens in prayer, or turn to the audience with a powerful gesture to emphasize a point. These movements help to make the story more vivid and emotionally resonant.

Panunuluyan

Panunuluyan is a dramatic and musical performance that reenacts Joseph and Mary’s search for shelter in Bethlehem on the night Jesus was born. The Tagalog word panunuluyan literally means “to seek shelter” or “to seek entry.”

Panunuluyan is traditionally performed on Christmas Eve, 24 December. In this dramatic reenactment, actors portraying Joseph and Mary go from house to house, asking for a place to stay so Mary can give birth to Jesus. Each time, they are turned away. Finally, they are offered a simple stable, which becomes the birthplace of Jesus.

Watch this excerpt of a panunuluyan performance. What stands out in this panunuluyan excerpt in terms of rhythm, pattern, and movements?

You will likely notice the gentle rhythm of the music. The panunuluyan uses slow, peaceful music that complements the narrative. As Mary and Joseph travel from house to house, the music maintains a calm, repetitive rhythm, underscoring the quiet determination of their journey.

Similar to the senakulo, the panunuluyan follows a clear and consistent pattern. The story always unfolds in the same way—Joseph and Mary knock on several doors seeking shelter, and they are repeatedly refused. Only the final house welcomes them, representing the humble stable where Jesus is born.

The movements in the panunuluyan are simple yet meaningful. The actors move deliberately and respectfully as they walk and knock on doors. They may bow politely or raise their hands in supplication. Each gesture is imbued with emotion, helping the audience to connect with the feelings of the characters.

Visual Arts: Rhythm, Pattern, and Movement in Design

Now let us talk about visual arts! Just like in performing arts, we can also find rhythm, pattern, and movement in drawings, paintings, sculptures, and other forms of visual expression. These elements help to organize the artwork and guide the viewer’s eye.

In visual art, rhythm refers to the repetition of elements like shapes, lines, or colors, which creates a visual “beat” or flow. This repetition makes your eyes move across the artwork, creating a feeling of motion or continuity.

Pattern occurs when these repeated elements—such as lines, colors, or shapes—are arranged in an organized and predictable way. Patterns help to make the artwork feel balanced, harmonious, and visually connected.

Movement in art does not mean the picture is actually moving. Instead, it is the way the artwork is designed to direct your eyes around the composition. Artists use various elements like lines, curves, shapes, and the arrangement of objects (space) to lead your gaze from one part of the artwork to another, creating a sense of visual journey.

Let us explore how these elements appear in some real works of art from our own country!

Palaspas

Palaspas are intricately woven palm fronds you often see in Catholic churches on Palm Sunday. Catholics bring these to church to reenact the scene where Jesus arrived in Jerusalem and was greeted by believers waving palm leaves. Some palaspas are simple, while others are ornately decorated with elaborate patterns and weaving.

Watch the video and learn about how to make different designs of palaspas. What do you notice about the designs of the palaspas? You will likely observe that there are many different styles, each one unique!

One common feature is the repetitive nature of the designs. The way the palm leaves are folded and woven often follows a steady, flowing sequence, much like a beat in music. Your eyes can follow the curves, loops, and various shapes, which gives the entire design a calm and balanced feel.

You will also easily spot clear patterns. The same shapes—like triangles, diamonds, or leaf-like forms—are used repeatedly. Some patterns are woven and dense, while others are more open and airy. These repeated shapes provide the palaspas with its distinct structure and intricate beauty.

Even though a palaspas doesn't physically move, some designs can evoke a sense of movement. The folds and lines might appear to swirl, flow, or point upward, guiding your eyes through the design, similar to how you follow steps in a dance.

Another important aspect to notice is the use of lines. The natural shape of the palm leaves, combined with how they are cut and folded, creates various types of lines—straight, zigzag, and curved. All these lines come together to form the intricate details and the overall shape of the design.

Parol

The parol is a traditional Filipino Christmas decoration. It is a star-shaped lantern that symbolizes the Star of Bethlehem and is widely displayed during the Christmas season.

Traditionally, the parol is made using bamboo sticks and colorful Japanese paper, but modern versions often use materials like capiz shells, plastic, or metal. Many contemporary parols also incorporate lights, allowing them to glow brightly at night.

A parol is more than just a Christmas decoration—it is a vibrant example of how rhythm, pattern, movement, and lines come together in visual art.

Rhythm in a parol can be seen in the repetition of shapes, such as the star’s points or the rays extending from its center. In modern parols, blinking lights also create a steady visual rhythm, making the lantern feel dynamic and full of life, perfectly capturing the festive spirit of Christmas.

This repetition also creates patterns. Most parol designs feature a central five-pointed star, surrounded by repeating elements like triangles, diamonds, or circles. Modern parols enhance this with lights that twinkle in set sequences, giving the design even more structure and visual interest. These patterns naturally guide your eye’s movement across the design. For example, a well-crafted parol often draws your gaze outward from its central point.

Finally, lines play a crucial role in shaping the parol. The bamboo frame provides strong, straight lines, while the cut paper, capiz shells, or plastic decorations create smooth curves and delicate edges. In a lighted parol, the glowing lines of the lights form new shapes and patterns, making the entire design appear almost magical.

48. The subli is a partner dance from Batangas (Bauan and Alitagtag), while the sinulog and ati-atihan are group-oriented. The male dancers keep a stooped posture, and the dance includes bowing, stepping, and turning in circles.
49. A central five-pointed star surrounded by repeating triangles, diamonds, or circles. Modern parols add lights that twinkle in set sequences, and the pattern draws the eye outward from the center.
50. Joseph and Mary go from house to house on Christmas Eve asking for shelter. They knock on several doors and are repeatedly refused; only the final house welcomes them, representing the humble stable where Jesus is born.
